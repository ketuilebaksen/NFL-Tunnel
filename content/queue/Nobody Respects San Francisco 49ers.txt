There is a prize in professional football that nobody wants to win.
 
Every spring, the NFL draft runs for three days and seven rounds. Two hundred and fifty-something names get called, and when the final one is announced, the room is mostly empty. The cameras are gone. The analysts have stopped analyzing. And the last man chosen gets handed a nickname that has followed the position for more than fifty years.
 
Mister Irrelevant.
 
It's meant kindly, sort of. There's a whole tradition attached to it — a week of celebrations in Newport Beach, California, a parade, a banquet, and a trophy. The trophy is called the Lowsman, and it's a joke about the Heisman: instead of a player carrying a football with his arm out, the Lowsman shows a player fumbling it. Dropping the ball. That's the statue they give you.
 
The history of that title is a history of men who disappeared. Most last men drafted never play a professional down. A few make a roster. Almost none of them matter.
 
On April 30th, 2022, the two hundred and sixty-second and final pick of the NFL draft was a quarterback from Iowa State named Brock Purdy.
 
Four years later, he signed a contract worth two hundred and sixty-five million dollars.
 
Two weeks into this season, one of the men who won a Super Bowl in that building called him the best quarterback in football right now.
 
And there are still people — a lot of people — who will tell you he isn't really that good.
 
This is the story of how the least important pick in a draft became the most argued-about quarterback in the sport. And of what it cost him along the way, which is more than most people remember.

Start with the father, because the father explains the son.
 
Shawn Purdy was a pitcher. He spent eight seasons in Minor League Baseball — eight years of long bus rides, small towns, motel rooms, and the specific kind of hope that a minor league season runs on. Eight years of being good enough to be paid, and never quite good enough to be called up.
 
He never reached the major leagues.
 
Think about what that does to a household. Brock Purdy did not grow up in a home where success was assumed. He grew up in a home where someone he loved had gotten close, worked as hard as a person can work, and still didn't get there. That's a very different education than the one a five-star recruit gets.
 
The family settled in Queen Creek, Arizona, in the desert southeast of Phoenix. Brock's older sister became a college softball pitcher. His younger brother, Chubba, became a quarterback too. Sports weren't a hobby in that house. They were the family language.
 
Brock went to Perry High School in Gilbert. He won there — thirty-nine starts as a quarterback, twenty-seven of them wins. He was named the state's player of the year by the Arizona Republic, and Gatorade named him the state's football player of the year too.
 
So he was, by any honest measure, one of the best high school quarterbacks in Arizona.
 
And the recruiting services rated him three stars.
 
Three stars, for anyone who doesn't follow college recruiting, means: fine. It means a mid-level program should take you and a big program shouldn't lose sleep over missing you. The quarterbacks who go on to be first-round picks are usually rated four or five.
 
Why three?
 
Because of what he looked like. He wasn't tall enough to impress anyone standing next to him. He wasn't fast in a straight line. His arm was good, not frightening. Nothing about Brock Purdy passed the airport test — the old scouting phrase for whether a player looks like a star when he walks through a terminal.
 
What he did have was the least measurable thing in the sport. He knew where everybody was. He processed a defense faster than the defense could disguise itself. And he had a temperament that never moved — not in a tied fourth quarter, not after an interception, not in front of ninety thousand people.
 
None of that shows up on a recruiting page.
 
In February 2018 he committed to Iowa State. Not Alabama, not Ohio State, not one of the programs that reload at quarterback every two years. Iowa State — a school in Ames, Iowa, with one of the hardest football histories in the country, a program that had spent decades being the team other Big 12 schools expected to beat.
 
That's where the best quarterback Arizona produced that year went to play.
 
He arrived third on the depth chart, which is exactly where a three-star freshman is supposed to arrive.
 
Then, in October of his freshman season, Iowa State put him in.
 
What happened next became the thing Iowa State fans still talk about. He came off the bench against Oklahoma State and threw for over three hundred yards in an upset. Then they gave him the job, and he kept it for four years.
 
Look at the arc:
 
As a freshman, eight starts, sixteen touchdowns, a passer rating that ranked sixth in the entire country.
 
As a sophomore, he led the Big 12 in passing yards — nearly four thousand of them — and in touchdowns.
 
As a junior, in 2020, he did something no Iowa State team had ever done. The Cyclones went nine and three and finished the season ranked ninth in the nation. Ninth. The highest final ranking in the history of the program.
 
As a senior, he led the conference in passing yards again and in completion percentage.
 
By the time he left, he held thirty-two school records. Eighty-one passing touchdowns. A hundred total. Three-time all-conference. And a communication studies degree, because he actually finished it.
 
That is a four-year résumé that, at a different school, in a different body, makes a man a first-round pick.
 
Instead, the evaluations went like this.
 
He's a product of the system. His arm won't survive NFL windows. He can't make the throws that require a special arm. He's not athletic enough to escape professional pressure. He's a backup. Maybe a career backup, if he's lucky.
 
And here's the honest part: those evaluations were not stupid. Every one of them was pointing at something real. Brock Purdy genuinely did not have elite arm strength. He genuinely was not a great athlete by NFL standards. If you build a quarterback out of measurements, he is a below-average professional prospect, and thirty-two teams looked at the measurements and reached the same conclusion.
 
The problem is that the thing he was best at cannot be measured in a gym.
 
Eight quarterbacks were drafted in 2022 before his name came up. Then nine. Then ten. The rounds went by. Day two ended. Day three started, and the picks got faster, and the quarterbacks kept going — guys with better arms, better legs, better tape from better programs.
 
By the time they got to the end of the seventh round, there was one pick left in the entire draft, and it belonged to the San Francisco 49ers.
 
Here's the part nobody planned.
 
San Francisco did not take him to play. They had spent an enormous amount of capital the year before to move up and draft a quarterback named Trey Lance — three first-round picks, the kind of trade a front office bets a decade on. They also still had Jimmy Garoppolo, a starter who had taken them to a Super Bowl.
 
Brock Purdy was the third quarterback. On most teams, the third quarterback never plays. His entire job is to run the scout team in practice and be available in case of catastrophe.
 
So the 2022 season needed a catastrophe. It got two.
 
In Week Two, Trey Lance broke his ankle. Season over, and effectively career over in San Francisco — the three-first-round-pick quarterback, gone in two games.
 
That moved Garoppolo back into the starting job, and for eleven weeks it worked. Then in Week Thirteen, against Miami, Garoppolo broke his foot.
 
And a rookie who had been drafted last, as a formality, jogged onto an NFL field with a team that was trying to win a championship.
 
What followed doesn't really have a comparison.
 
He won. Then he won again. He won every single one of his five regular-season starts.
 
The one everybody remembers came on December 11th, 2022, in his first career start. The opponent was Tom Brady's Tampa Bay Buccaneers. The final score was thirty-five to seven. Brock Purdy became the only quarterback in the history of the league to beat Tom Brady in a first career start — and the first player ever given the Mister Irrelevant title to throw a touchdown pass in the regular season.
 
The numbers from those five games: sixty-seven percent completions, thirteen touchdowns, four interceptions, a passer rating over a hundred and seven. From a seventh-round rookie in December, running a playoff offense.
 
Then came the playoffs, and he kept going.
 
Wild card round against Seattle: three hundred and thirty-two yards, three touchdown passes, one on the ground. Forty-one to twenty-three.
 
Divisional round against Dallas: nineteen to twelve, a grinding, cold, defensive game that he managed without a mistake.
 
And so, three months after being the last name called in the draft, Brock Purdy was starting an NFC Championship Game.
 
He would last one quarter.
 
January 29th, 2023. Philadelphia.
 
In the first quarter, Purdy dropped back to pass and a defender came down on his throwing arm as he released.
 
Complete tear of the UCL in his right elbow. Not a sprain. Not a strain. The ligament that lets a human being throw a football at high velocity, torn all the way through, in the first quarter of a game that decides who goes to the Super Bowl.
 
He went to the locker room. The 49ers brought in Josh Johnson, a veteran journeyman, and Johnson took a hit, got a concussion, and had to come out too.
 
Which left San Francisco with a problem that almost never happens in professional football: they were playing in a conference championship game, and they had nobody who could throw a pass.
 
So Brock Purdy came back out. He could not throw. Everyone in the stadium knew he could not throw — the Eagles knew, the broadcast knew, the fans knew. He stood in the huddle, took the snap, and handed the football to a running back. Over and over. Because the alternative was forfeiting.
 
Philadelphia won thirty-one to seven.
 
That game is remembered as an embarrassment, and it was. But watch it again as a story about a person, and it becomes one of the strangest images in modern playoff history: a rookie who was drafted last in the world, standing on the biggest stage of his life, holding a football he was physically unable to throw, refusing to leave the field.
 
In the spring, he had surgery on the elbow. And this is the part people forget when they argue about him on the internet.
 
A UCL repair means you do not throw for months. For a pitcher, this injury is career-defining. For a quarterback, it means the most basic action of the job — the motion he'd been repeating since he was a kid in Arizona — has to be rebuilt from the joint outward. Strength first. Then short throws on a knee. Then distance. Then velocity. Then, finally, throwing while someone is trying to hit you.
 
He did that rehab through the entire offseason. He was not fully cleared until close to training camp.
 
And nobody, including San Francisco, actually knew what kind of quarterback would come out the other side.
 
2023 answered the question in the loudest possible way, and somehow the argument only got worse.
 
He started sixteen games and won twelve of them. Four thousand two hundred and eighty passing yards — a San Francisco franchise record, breaking a mark that had stood since 2000. Thirty-one touchdowns. And the best passer rating in the National Football League: one hundred and thirteen point zero.
 
Not a good number for a young quarterback. The best number in the league, at any age.
 
Some individual nights from that season still look like typos. Against Arizona, he completed twenty of twenty-one passes. Against Tampa Bay, twenty-one of twenty-five for three hundred and thirty-three yards and three touchdowns, with a perfect passer rating — the first 49ers quarterback to do that since Joe Montana in 1989.
 
He went to the Pro Bowl as the starter for the conference. He finished near the top of the MVP voting.
 
In the playoffs, he beat Green Bay with a late drive in the cold. Then, in the NFC Championship Game against Detroit, his team fell behind by seventeen points at halftime — and he brought them back to win it, thirty-four to thirty-one.
 
Super Bowl fifty-eight was Kansas City. He threw for two hundred and fifty-five yards, and the game went to overtime, and San Francisco lost twenty-five to twenty-two.
 
That's one year removed from being handed the Lowsman Trophy.
 
And here is what happened next in the public conversation, which is the real story of Brock Purdy's career: none of it counted.
 
The phrase that attached itself to him was "system quarterback." The argument went that Kyle Shanahan's offense is so well designed that it manufactures open receivers, and that Purdy was simply the man standing there when the scheme did its work. Put anybody in that offense, the argument said, and they'd look like this.
 
You can make that case. Shanahan's offense really is one of the great tactical constructions in the sport, and it really has made average quarterbacks look better than they are.
 
But there's a counter-argument, and it's just as real. That offense existed before him, with other quarterbacks, and it never produced the best passer rating in football. The throws he makes off-platform — falling away, on the run, arm angle dropped — are not scheme. They're a person. And a system that can be executed by anybody would not need the man executing it to be the fourth-best quarterback in an MVP vote.
 
The debate was never really settled. It still isn't. It's the defining feature of his career: a quarterback who keeps producing results, and keeps having to prove that the results were his.

Then came the years that tested every part of it.
 
2024 was the collapse. San Francisco went six and eleven and missed the playoffs. The roster fell apart around him — Christian McCaffrey, Brandon Aiyuk, Trent Williams, Nick Bosa, all of them missing time. Purdy himself played through a shoulder and an elbow problem and missed two games. His numbers slid: twenty touchdowns, twelve interceptions, a passer rating of ninety-six.
 
Every critic who had spent a year saying the offense was carrying him pointed at that season and said: there it is.
 
And then San Francisco did something that tells you what the people who watch him every day actually believe.
 
On May 20th, 2025, the 49ers gave Brock Purdy a five-year extension worth two hundred and sixty-five million dollars.
 
Sit with that. The final pick of a draft — a player whose entire first contract was worth a rounding error by NFL standards, who was taken as a formality, who was given a trophy of a man dropping a football — became one of the highest-paid athletes in the world.
 
Then the 2025 season started, and his body took the year away from him.
 
Week One against Seattle. The 49ers won the game. But Purdy came out of it with two injuries at once — a shoulder problem and turf toe.
 
Turf toe again. The same injury that had turned Patrick Mahomes into a sitting target in a Super Bowl. For a quarterback, it attacks the one thing every throw begins with: the push off the ground.
 
He missed two games. Came back in Week Four against Jacksonville — and re-injured it in that game.
 
Then he missed six more.
 
Eight games. Half a season, watching a backup run his offense, on a contract that had just made him one of the highest-paid players in the sport, with the entire league's commentary class having a very loud opinion about whether he was worth it.
 
He has since talked about what that stretch did to him, and his answer was not what you'd expect from a man in that position. He said maybe it was God's way of making him sit back and see the big picture. He said watching someone else operate in that offense actually taught him things about it. And then he said the part that mattered.
 
The chip's back on the shoulder.
 
He returned in Week Eleven against Arizona. From that point on, San Francisco went six and one and scored thirty-one points a game — top five in the league over that stretch. In December he threw five touchdown passes in a single game against Indianapolis, a career high, and put up five total touchdowns the following week against Chicago.
 
In the wild card round, on the road in Philadelphia — the same building where his elbow had come apart three years earlier — he threw two interceptions and won anyway, twenty-three to nineteen.
 
The next week, Seattle beat San Francisco forty-one to six, and the season was over.
 
That's the shape of his career in one paragraph: extraordinary, injured, doubted, extraordinary again.
 
Which brings us to right now.
 
This offseason, San Francisco decided to stop asking their quarterback to do it with a thin roster. They signed Mike Evans — a receiver with more than a decade of thousand-yard seasons, a Super Bowl ring, and a body built for exactly the throws Purdy wasn't being given. They traded for Osa Odighizuwa, one of the most disruptive interior defensive linemen in football, to fix a pass rush that had collapsed. And the team that everyone outside the building described as old, expensive and breakable started the season looking like something else entirely.
 
Week One, against the Rams: twenty-seven to seven. Purdy threw three touchdown passes.
 
Week Two, against Miami: twenty completions on twenty-two attempts. Ninety-one percent. Two hundred and eighty-seven yards, two touchdown passes, and a touchdown of his own on the ground. Thirty-five to thirteen.
 
That game put him in the record book in a way nobody had managed before: no player in the history of the National Football League had ever completed ninety percent of his passes, thrown for two hundred and eighty-five yards with multiple touchdowns, and run for a touchdown in the same game.
 
And there's a number underneath that one that matters even more. Through his first five seasons, Brock Purdy now has more games with a passer rating above a hundred and forty than any quarterback in league history has had in their first five years — more than Ben Roethlisberger, more than Kurt Warner, more than Roger Staubach.
 
Mike Evans, who has caught passes in this league for twelve years, said he'd never played with a quarterback who threw it like this. A Super Bowl champion on television called him the MVP of the season through two weeks.
 
Two and oh. A hundred and nine career touchdowns' worth of receiver on the outside. A rebuilt defensive line. And a quarterback who is twenty-six years old and has already been to a Super Bowl, torn a ligament in his throwing elbow, lost half a season to a toe, signed a quarter-billion-dollar contract, and spent every single week of it being told he's a product of his surroundings.
 
So what is the honest verdict on Brock Purdy?
 
I think it's this. Football's evaluation system is built to measure what a body can do — how far the arm throws, how fast the legs run, how tall a man stands in a doorway. It is very good at that. What it cannot measure is the part of quarterbacking that happens in the two seconds before the throw, and that part is where he lives.
 
Thirty-two teams looked at him in 2022 and saw the limits. They were not wrong about the limits. They were wrong about what the limits were worth.
 
And the nickname turned out to be the most inaccurate thing anybody has ever said about him. They called him irrelevant on the last night of that draft, when the room had already emptied out and the cameras had gone home.
 
Four years later, he's the argument the entire league is still having.
