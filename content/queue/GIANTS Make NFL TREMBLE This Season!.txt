A football season can end in seven plays.

Monday night, week two. The New York Giants had just beaten Dallas eight days earlier, and for the first time in a long time there was a reason to believe in this franchise — a second-year quarterback who looked like the answer, a receiver back from a torn knee, a new head coach with seventeen years of credibility behind him.

Seven snaps into that Monday night game, it was over.

Third down. Jaxson Dart dropped back, found his target, and released the ball. Two Rams defenders closed on him from opposite sides at the same moment, and as he followed through, his left knee buckled underneath him.

He walked off under his own power. That's the detail that fooled everybody, including the people in the building. During the broadcast, a camera caught a Giants trainer telling the head coach the injury looked like four to six weeks. After the game, the coach said his quarterback had avoided the devastating outcome — that it was in the neighborhood of a sprain.

Then came the MRI.

Damage to the MCL. Damage to the PCL. Damage to the meniscus. The ACL — the one everybody fears — was intact, and the doctors expected a full recovery. But before the players, the coach — because none of the rest of this makes sense without him.

John Harbaugh spent nearly two decades as the head coach of the Baltimore Ravens. He won a Super Bowl there. He took teams to the playoffs with four different quarterbacks, in four different eras of offensive football, and the thing people in the sport respect him for is not his scheme. It's that his teams are never, under any circumstances, easy to play.

That is a specific kind of coaching, and it's rarer than it sounds. A Harbaugh team wins games in which nothing works. It wins when the quarterback is backup-level, when the weather is bad, when the margin is three points and it's fourth quarter and the stadium is hostile. He has done it with young quarterbacks, with veteran quarterbacks, and with quarterbacks nobody had heard of two weeks earlier.

Now put that résumé next to this season. A team loses its young franchise quarterback in September and then wins a twelve-to-seven game, and a thirty-six to twenty-four street fight that ended on a defensive touchdown.

Those are not accidents. That is the signature of a building where losing the quarterback does not mean losing the season — because the season was never built on the quarterback alone.
